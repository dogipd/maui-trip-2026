🤿 Optional: DIY Beginner Shore Snorkel (Non-Swimmer Friendly)

Safer alternative to the skipped offshore Molokini trip. Shore entry lets you stand up any time and set your own pace — the right way for a nervous swimmer to try the reef. Morning only, protected beach, never alone, bail if it's choppy.

  • Gear (Xplorie pass): Rent mask, fins & a snorkel VEST (not a bulky life jacket — a vest lets you float face-down) plus a dry-top snorkel at Auntie Snorkel — 2439 S Kihei Rd, Ste 101-A, Kihei (across from Kamaole Beach I; ~10 min south of Māʻalaea). From $17.95; 2-for-1 freebie if booking 2+ items online.
  • Best beginner beaches (South Maui): Ulua Beach (Wailea) — small protected cove, sandy entry, reef on the left (top pick); Maluaka / "Turtle Town" shore (Mākena) — calm, sandy, turtles near shore; Mōkapu Beach — gentle, steps from the Andaz (Oct 30–Nov 1).
  • Avoid for a first try: Black Rock (deeper entry/jumpers) and any beach with visible surf or rocky entry.

✅ Safety checklist (do every time):

  • ☐ Morning only — calmest, glassiest water before the wind builds.
  • ☐ Never alone — buddy up with each other at all times.
  • ☐ Check conditions day-of — scan the beach for shorebreak/current; check hawaiibeachsafety.com for the beach's hazard rating & warnings. Visible surf = don't go.
  • ☐ Practice in the shallows first — stand in waist/chest-deep water, face down, breathe through the snorkel a few minutes before drifting out.
  • ☐ Stay parallel to shore & close in — don't chase turtles out to sea; keep sand under you.
  • ☐ Current check — if you're kicking but shore isn't getting closer, you're in a current: float on your back (vest holds you), signal, swim parallel to shore, not straight in.
  • ☐ Mask fit & defog — a leaky/foggy mask is the #1 reason first-timers quit; test fit at the shop.
  • ☐ Reef-safe sunscreen + rash guard (already on packing list).

Note: Drowning is the leading cause of visitor death in Hawaii and snorkeling is overrepresented — almost always panic in deep/rough water, not strong swimmers. These rules are what keep it a relaxing swim instead of a scare.

Understanding Hawaiian Canoe Crops

Ancient voyagers brought living "canoe plants" across the Pacific that formed the bedrock of Hawaiian culinary culture:

  • Kalo (Taro): The sacred root of Hawaii. The corm is steamed and mashed with water to make poi; young leaves are braised in coconut cream as lūʻau.
  • ʻUlu (Breadfruit): A large, starchy tree fruit that tastes like freshly baked potatoes or bread. Look for warm roasted ʻulu or crunchy ʻulu chips at roadside stands in Keʻanae (Day 5) and Olowalu (Day 3)!
  • ʻUala (Sweet Potato): Vivid purple-fleshed tuber that brings natural sweetness to luau plates and local spreads.
